Prices Calendar — A/B Usability Test

Static vs. Dynamic cheapest-day highlighting · Unmoderated, between-subjects · Useberry + Prolific
16 participants · 4 per arm · Desktop & Mobile · 13–18 August 2026

Nothing failed. Every participant completed every task they attempted — 16/16 on date-picking and 8/8 on the price-trends exploration. Most finished Task 1 in under 30 seconds. Neither behaviour is a blocker, so this is a question of preference and comprehension, not usability failure.
The dynamic version scored higher on confidence, not lower. On desktop, Version B users averaged 4.25/5 confidence that they'd picked genuinely cheap days, against 2.75/5 for Version A. The distributions tell the story better than the means: A's four scores were 1, 2, 3, 5 — spread across the entire scale — while B's were 4, 4, 4, 5. This contradicts the hypothesis that stable green days would feel more trustworthy.
The strongest finding isn't A vs B at all. Three participants, independently and across both arms and both platforms, asked for the same missing thing: the actual price on each day in the calendar. The calendar tells people which days are cheap but never how much — and that gap drove more confusion than the static/dynamic difference did.

This is the reversal worth paying attention to. Desktop A's individual scores were 1, 2, 3, 5 — no consensus whatsoever. Desktop B's were 4, 4, 4, 5. Watching the price react to their choice appears to have reassured B users that the system was working on their behalf. A users got no such feedback, and three of the four came away unsure they'd actually found a good deal — despite rating the task itself a perfect 5/5 for ease.
Easy and reassuring are not the same thing. Version A is the cleanest example in the study: a unanimous 5.00 for ease alongside the lowest confidence of any arm. People found the static calendar effortless to operate and still didn't trust the outcome. Ease-of-use metrics alone would have hidden this entirely.

The trends view is the most reassuring surface in the product. Within Version A, confidence goes from 2.75 after the calendar task to 4.25 after the trends task — the same people, the same session, a 1.5-point jump. The calendar left them unsure; the trends graph convinced them. That is a strong argument for giving trends more prominence, and it is measured within-subject rather than across arms.
Task 2 costs more effort, even though everyone succeeds. Average ease falls from 4.9 on Task 1 to 3.8 on Task 2, median time roughly doubles, and median clicks rise threefold in A (6.5 → 20.5) and almost ninefold in B (5.0 → 44.5). Version B users worked considerably harder — 162 s and 45 clicks at the median against A's 51 s and 21 — and rated it slightly harder (3.50 vs 4.00). The task is solvable by everyone; the friction is in discovery and navigation.
When people find it, it works exactly as designed. One B participant navigated the view precisely as intended and reported back the numbers the prototype is built around — August at €130, March the cheapest at €110.

06Did people notice the behaviour?

The central question: is moving green perceived, and how does it feel?

ArmPerceptionReaction
A — Static
green should NOT move		 Correctly perceived as stable. Most said they simply didn't notice any movement — which is the accurate answer. Invisible Stability works in the background. Nobody praised it because nobody saw it — and nobody drew confidence from it either.
B — Dynamic
green DOES move		 8/8 noticed something changed — the movement is unmistakable on both platforms. Split 4 read it as the system finding them a better deal; 3 were unsettled or felt misled; 1 neutral.
The movement itself isn't the problem — the unexplained movement is. Not one B participant failed because green moved. The negative reactions were all about not knowing why: whether the price was per-leg or total, whether the new green days were better or just different. Version B needs to narrate what it's doing, not stop doing it.

09What to do next

1 · Put prices on the days Highest impact

The single most requested change, raised independently by three participants across both arms and platforms — and the most plausible explanation for Version A's 2.75 confidence score. Green currently makes a promise the interface never substantiates. Even small per-day figures would let people verify the signal instead of trusting it blindly. On desktop this exists only as a hover tooltip; on mobile there is nothing at all.

2 · Resolve the per-leg vs. total ambiguity Blocking for B

The hardest session in the study (113 s, 108 clicks, rated 2/5) came down to one unanswered question: is "Under €X" the price of one flight or both? Version B's dynamic pricing sharpens this ambiguity, because the number visibly moves. Label it explicitly.

3 · Reposition the disclaimer — don't rewrite it Medium

Four of eight desktop participants never saw it; all four who did found it helpful. That is as clean a placement problem as you'll get. Changing the copy would be solving the wrong thing.

4 · Promote the price-trends view Strong evidence

This is now backed by a within-subject measurement, which is the most reliable signal in the study: the same Version A participants went from 2.75 confidence after the calendar to 4.25 after the trends view. A participant independently suggested trends should be the primary surface. It answers the question people actually have — when should I go? — but currently sits behind a secondary tab and costs three to nine times more clicks to reach.

5 · Keep the dynamic behaviour Verdict

B matched A on completion (both perfect) and beat it decisively on confidence, with far more consistent scores. Its problem is silence, not motion: when green moves without explanation, a minority find it unsettling. Narrate the recalculation — a brief "cheapest returns for your dates" — and the friction likely disappears. The evidence does not support reverting to static.

10How much to trust this

Four participants per arm. These are directional signals, not statistics. A single person moves any mean by 0.25–0.5 points, so no difference reported here would survive a significance test, and none is claimed to. What the study is good for is surfacing which questions matter — and on that it delivered clearly: the missing prices, the per-leg ambiguity and the confidence gap all showed up repeatedly and independently.
  • The confidence gap is the most robust finding — it appears in both the means (2.75 vs 4.25) and the distributions (1,2,3,5 vs 4,4,4,5), and is corroborated by the within-subject jump in Version A between Task 1 and Task 2.
  • Mobile arms tested Task 1 only — there is no price-trends view on mobile, so all Task 2 findings are desktop-only.
  • Confidence wasn't captured for mobile B or for Task 2 in Version B, which limits how far the confidence comparison can be pushed.
  • Self-reported ease clusters at the ceiling — 13 of 16 gave Task 1 a 5, including all four Version A desktop users who then reported low confidence. Ease and trust must be measured separately.
  • One comment reflects a prototype limit (pre-filled dates) rather than the design under test.
  • Next step: 12–15 per arm would tell you whether B's confidence advantage is real. At n=4 it rests on four people.
